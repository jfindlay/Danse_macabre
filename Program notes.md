# Danse macabre, op. 40 — for unaccompanied violin

## 1. The programme

Henri Cazalis's poem (1872), which Saint-Saëns first set as a song and then rewrote as this tone
poem, opens:

> *Zig et zig et zag, la mort en cadence*\
> *Frappant une tombe avec son talon,*\
> *La mort à minuit joue un air de danse,*\
> *Zig et zig et zag, sur son violon.*

Midnight strikes. Death sits on a tombstone and tunes his fiddle — the E string pulled down to E♭,
so that the first thing he plays is the tritone A–E♭, the medieval *diabolus in musica*. The dead
climb out and dance: a waltz, with its ingenious syncopations, first wheedling in the flute, then a
sweeping second tune in the fiddle itself, then the two together. The xylophone rattles their bones.
The old plainchant of the dead, the *Dies irae*, is mocked in a bright major key over a
Spanish-sounding strum. The dance whirls through a fugue, an unexpected oasis in B major, a
chromatic scale that climbs two octaves and falls back, hammer-blows, and a long drive to a
shrieking climax. Then a horn holds one cold note, the oboe crows — *le coq a chanté* — and the
dancers scatter. Death's fiddle mutters its last lines alone over the horns, the tune's first notes
flicker upward through the strings as the last of them vanish, and the piece ends on two soft
plucked chords.

Saint-Saëns wrote to his publisher in 1876 that violinists no longer wanted their top string tuned
any way but in E♭. The tuning is the piece's signature, and it is the one thing this arrangement
could not have done without.

The piece was built from material that was already folklore — the medieval *danse macabre*, the
*Dies irae* chant, the waltz — so a version for one fiddle is less a reduction than a return: it
closes a loop the composer opened.

## 2. The premise

**The occasion.** A single violinist, in costume, at a Halloween carnival or street festival. The
listener is unfamiliar with the piece, hears it from across a street, and will catch a portion of
one violin line. The arrangement is written for that ear: mysterious, virtuosic, and — where the
original is sublime — sublime. Every choice below was tested against *what would that listener
take in?* rather than *what would the score-reader miss?* The performer will hear Saint-Saëns's
whole orchestra in their head; the audience will not, and the arrangement does not pretend they
will.

**Two chairs.** *Danse macabre* is a symphonic poem with an extended solo, not a concerto: in the
orchestra the "solo violin" is the leader standing up to play Death, a character inside the
ensemble rather than a protagonist against it. The part lives on the concertmaster–soloist plane,
and the one violin here plays both chairs. As soloist it plays Death's part untouched; as
concertmaster it plays the section's own line, which is why the first-violin part is the spine of
nearly every bar in which the solo is silent, and the winds and brass are lifted only where they
carry something the strings do not.

**The source.** The full orchestral score (provided by [Le
Concert](https://web.archive.org/web/20231001212827/https://le-concert.pagesperso-orange.fr/st-saens.html)),
not the composer's violin-and-piano reduction: the reduction's violin line is a duo part that
already absorbs orchestral material, and I wanted to make those choices myself.  Indeed, this is a
project I had been planning for ~30 years. Bar numbers throughout are identical to the full score's
for which the solo-violin part only plays about 130.

**Three rules, applied bar by bar.** The arrangement was made under these constraints:

1. *Preserve.* Every note of the orchestral solo-violin part is immutable — pitch, rhythm,
   articulation, dynamics. It is Saint-Saëns's own writing for this instrument in this tuning.
2. *Lift.* Where the solo rests, the violin plays the orchestra's prevailing line, sometimes in a
   different octave. Where the orchestra doubles that line in octaves and the violin can, the violin
   plays octaves.
3. *Fill.* Only where nothing liftable exists does the arrangement add harmony or counter-material,
   and then in a named idiom. In practice this rule almost never fires: the orchestra always has a
   line to give.

**Fidelity of form.** 477 bars of 3/4, the rehearsal marks A–N at the source's bar numbers, no
cuts, no insertions, no free-rhythm passages. The build enforces this mechanically (§5).

**Two deliberate exceptions to rule 1**, both performer's-perspective calls. At 118/120/126/128 the
solo's tenuto downbeats are plucked, and at 469 the solo's final tremolo gives way to the
orchestra's last figures. The reasons are in §3 (Episodes 5 and 18).

**Composer-sanctioned colours.** The score itself says *À défaut de harpe — pizz.* at bar 33, and
its strings go *col legno* under the xylophone at 129. The arrangement takes those two
instructions as licence for the whole piece: pizzicato stands for the harp and for the string
section's own pizzicato, *col legno battuto* for the xylophone's bones. Each extended technique
carries exactly one orchestral identity.

**The pizzicato is liberal on purpose.** The first third of the piece is full of harp and
full-section pizzicato in the source, so plucked colour is textually honest; and the rapid
alternation of bow and pluck — a four-string chord plucked on the beat and the bow back on the
string an eighth later — is the Sarasate surprise (*Zigeunerweisen*'s left-hand pizzicato) that
makes a street audience wonder how it was done.

**Known themes resonate.** Once a tune has been heard enough times to be carried, a further
restatement of it in the orchestra may yield to simultaneous *novel* material in the lift; the
known figure sounds unplayed in the listener's mind. This is a tie-breaker between two liftable
lines, never a licence to add.

**The tableau principle.** Saint-Saëns's piece quotes history — plainchant, a Bach-style fugato,
the waltz — and the arrangement makes each quotation legible in solo-violin terms rather than
replicating the orchestral texture: the fugato reads as a Bach sonata fugue (entry cues as
double-stops, single-line episodes between), the whirl against the brass theme reads as Ysaÿe's
*Obsession* and Ernst's *Erlkönig* (the theme as the bass of the figuration), the harp reads as
pizzicato. Allusion is the arranger's licence; replication is not the goal.

**Lineage.** The models consulted, and what each gave: Paganini — Caprice 9's *imitando il
flauto* for the flute tune, and the register of the summits; Sarasate, Saint-Saëns's own
collaborator — the pluck-and-retake surprise and the *Carmen Fantasy*'s virtuoso register; Ysaÿe's
*Obsession* and Ernst's *Erlkönig* — a theme carried as the bass of running figuration, and the
solo-violin death ballad as a genre; Bach — compound melody, unaccompanied violin polyphony;
Berlioz's *Ronde du sabbat* — the fugato's ancestor. Enescu's *doina* was held in reserve for the
coda and never spent: Death's *déclamé* is already a *doina* in Saint-Saëns's hand, and the
spectral and the kinetic languages belong in different episodes — the piece's power is character
contrast. One craft rule from the same survey governs §4: a great player's idiosyncrasies are
performance nuance, not notation; what transfers to paper is the notated fingerprint —
figuration, chord spacing, bowing — and those cost no licence.

## 3. The dramaturgy, episode by episode

Marks: A 65 · B 117 · C 137 · D 173 · E 205 · F 237 · G 253 · H 273 · I 309 · K 362 · L 394 ·
*poco stringendo* 409 · *Animato* 416 · M 430 · *Tempo 1°* 438 · N 467 · end 477.

### 1. Midnight (1–24)

The harp strikes twelve Ds, one a bar, over a horn pedal on the same note; the strings hold a
D-major shimmer twice (5–8, 13–16); then cellos and basses pluck a crooked little line that ends on
E♭. The violin plucks all twelve strokes with the left hand on the open D, *f*, let ring — the
harp's, the horn's and the open string's D are one pitch — with the bow in hand from bar 1 so it
can draw the shimmer, *ppp sul tasto*, over the continuing toll: a single tied *D5* (octaves in
scientific pitch notation, middle C = C4) the first time, the first violins' own *D5–F♯5* pair the
second. Bar 17 is silent in every voice and stays silent. The bass line is plucked an octave up.
Nothing is improvised over the clock: the twelve strokes
are programme and must stay countable, and a melodic utterance before bar 25 would spend the
entrance. The first *voiced* bow stroke in the piece is Death tuning up.

### 2. The summons and the first waltz tune (25–48)

The solo's tritone summons, verbatim. Its last stab (32.3) and the downbeat after it are the
string section's plucked D-major and G-minor chords, so the summons closes with two full pizzicato
chords — the same two chords will close the piece. Then the flute's waltz tune is lifted at its
own octave, *p*, *sulla tastiera, imitando il flauto* (Paganini's Caprice 9 device), unaccompanied,
with an optional left-hand-pizzicato open G marking the bass; and the first violins' answer at the
octave above, *pp*, with their own trills, over an optional open-D drone.

### 3. The second tune, *largamente* (49–64)

The solo's own broad descending theme, verbatim, over the pizzicato strings' bass G as an
optional left-hand click. No counter-voices: the first statement is clean.

### 4. Exchanges on the first tune (65–116, [A])

Oboe and bassoon toss the tune's head in D major; the solo answers *marcatissimo*; the first
violins take the tune *f* over tutti chords; the solo again, *ff*, on the second string; then the
whole string section plays the second tune *largamente* with the winds stabbing on beats 2 and 3.
The violin lifts the oboe at its own octave with the solo's immutable downbeat under it, plays the
solo verbatim, and gives the first violins' statement the tutti chord on its downbeats — plucked,
the bow back for the next eighth (the Sarasate surprise, first instance). The *largamente* is the
first-violin line with the winds' stab as a struck double-stop on beats 2 and 3 and the open G
under the bass bars: the thing that makes the tutti statement different from the solo's.

### 5. Fragments and bones (117–136, [B])

A strict four-bar antiphony: tutti D-major stamps, the solo's tune in D major, then the wind choir
and xylophone with the tune in G minor over plucked and *col legno* string chords, and the solo's
*ff* open-string chords. The stamps are plucked D-major chords; the solo's phrase is verbatim with
its tenuto downbeats plucked under a D (the second deliberate breach of rule 1 — textural
variety, and the surprise); the xylophone statements are *col legno battuto* at the piccolo's
octave with the string chords as battuto double-stops under them, thickening the second time as
the orchestra does. The violin becomes the section for four bars. Known loss: true *f* and some
pitch clarity under the wood — accepted, it is the tune's fourth hearing in forty bars.

### 6. Fugato (137–172, [C])

Berlioz's *Ronde du sabbat* is the ancestor: a staccato dance-fugue for the dead at the programme's
midpoint, five entries seven bars apart on the second tune's head, each with a running staccato
counter-line, ending in a wind sweep up two octaves. On one violin, Bach's solo-violin polyphony
is the model — compound melody, voices present as double-stops at structural points and as
registral alternation between. Entry 1 alone, as the orchestra has it. Entries 2 and 3 are held
whole in one voice with the counter-line in the other, as far as the hand allows; entries 4 and 5
are the counter-line as surface with the subject's upbeat cued in double-stops. The register
climbs entry by entry as the orchestra's does, and the final sweep is taken an octave above the
flute — at the piccolo's sounding pitch — to the top of the E♭ string, so that 170–173 is one
ascent and the cliff down to *p* at [D] is the larger.

### 7. Skeletons (173–204, [D])

One sixteen-bar strain stated twice, a fourth apart, E Phrygian over a pedal: guitar-strum thirds
in the winds on beats 1 and 3, the strings' semitone turn as a round, a cymbal on beat 2. The
winds' tune at 181–185 is the *Dies irae* — its only appearance in the piece, in a bright major
key, which is the joke. The violin plays the strain as staccato sixths with the tune on top (the
source's thirds inverted so the headline is the top voice; the lower note is still a lift), beat 2
alternating a plucked open A (the cymbal) and the strings' bowed turn by the strain's own
hypermetre. In the second statement the orchestra's own first violins go pizzicato and the winds
add a sighing horn-call on 2–3: the violin plucks the strain on the downbeat with the open-A pedal
under it and bows the call, in octaves from 198 as the winds pile on, *cresc.* to *f* at 204. The
tune hockets between pluck and bow; that is the texture.

### 8. The oasis (205–236, [E])

The second tune in B major, four elided eight-bar statements: solo, flutes and horn, solo, violas
and second violins, over four solo cellos and a harp figure. The solo's statements are verbatim,
with the cellos' sustained notes as a lower voice wherever one sits on the string below the tune.
The orchestra's two answers are octave-doubled in the source, so the violin plays them in
octaves. The open E♭ string is the D♯ of B major: it rings inside the chord at 221.1 — Death's
tritone string is the sweet note of the oasis. The inner voice's D♯–D♮–D♯ is marked at its change
points as a three-note chord.

### 9. Spiky (237–252, [F])

A continuous two-octave chromatic scale, split between two desks of first violins, climbs and
falls twice; then the first tune's head in D♯ minor is tossed between strings and winds in canon
over a bass that walks down by semitones to G. The scale is one line, slurred a beat to a bow. The
canon is the first violins verbatim, their rests kept — the rests are the spikes — with the
tuba's bass dot in the three downbeat rests, so the chromatic drive into [G] is audible.

### 10. The whirl against the theme (253–272, [G])

First violins alone whirl up and down a two-octave arpeggio in triplets while the brass intone
the second tune's head *tenuto* three times. The brass articulate exactly where the whirl
articulates its bass, so the theme's note replaces the whirl's lowest note on beats 1 and 3 —
the Chaconne's device, the theme as a chord tone inside the figuration — and the upbeats ride the
held bass as double-stops. From 265 the German sixth is spelled out: the strings' accented C♯ is
the bottom of every E♭ bar. Positions climb with the crescendo.

### 11. Hammer-blows, chorale, wedge, wind (273–308, [H])

Four lifts in a row. 273–280: winds and strings alternate bars of marcato hammer-blows — German
sixth against D, diminished seventh against C — and the violin plays every bar, the dissonances as
four-string chords with the open E♭ on top, the resolutions as three. 281–288: the winds' chorale,
doubled at the octave in the source, as descending octaves to *p*. 289–296: the strings' wedge —
treble rising, bass falling — as double-stops that open from a second to an octave, then the
D-major vamp with the timpani's repeated A as the open string. 297–308: the winds' two-octave
chromatic descent, twice, the second time an octave higher. The first violins' *leggiero* figure
under those runs is the first tune's head on its sixth and seventh hearings; the listener carries
it, and the novel line wins.

### 12. Summons, stamps, two tunes (309–337, [I])

The solo returns with the tritone summons, then a gruff low stamp on D♭, C, B♭ while the strings
pluck waltz chords in its rests, then the second tune *espressivo* over the first tune's head in
the first violins. The solo is verbatim throughout; the orchestra is layered *over* it for the
first time: the wind chord above each stamp as a double-stop (a tritone over the D♭ — the
summons's interval a semitone down), the plucked waltz chords voiced so that each one's bass is
the solo's next stamp, and the first violins' cell lifted whole in the solo's rests and shown by
its tails in the two-beat breaths. The espressivo statement is kept intact: it is the last
lyrical moment before the hundred-bar drive, and the shadow-cells under it would have shortened
it.

### 13. The long string phrase (338–361)

The strings' one long chromatic phrase over a D pedal, climbing by half-steps to a peak and then
oscillating F♯°–G minor in a six-bar crescendo, while the winds hammer the first tune's head in
C minor against it. The phrase is the first-violin line, *pp*, the solo's held D under its first
bars as a chord; octaves from 350, where the orchestra doubles. The winds' figure is held back
until the strings take it at 354 — the sweep of the phrase completes first. Then the tutti
D7(♭9) hit as the open D and A under the first note of the first violins' non-legato descent from
the top of the E♭ string: the summons interval A–E♭, recalled at the orchestra's arrival.

### 14. Runs and tutti (362–393, [K])

The first-violin part is the spine and the tutti folds into its rests and downbeats: the brass
D7 stab as a three-string chord on each downbeat eighth of the landing cell; the winds' rise from
the open D up to a four-string G-minor crash; the first tune *ff* with the chord on the odd
downbeats, its tail at the first violins' own octave — *A6*, then a three-octave drop to the open
A for the basses' rise; the second statement in sixths and octaves over the winds' hammered B♭;
the strings-alone vamp bare; the winds' return as octaves and the four-string D7 stab into [L].

### 15. Ornamented (394–415, [L])

The one line in the orchestra is the first violins' — a descending chromatic figure in slurred
pairs with an upper grace-note before each, then turns and a D-major arpeggio, stated twice with
a tutti hammer between, then arpeggio sweeps over a D pedal, *poco stringendo*. It is violin
writing already and appears nowhere else in the piece, so it is lifted verbatim; the two tutti
hammers become chords in the hand the line is already in; and the last sweep is taken an octave
above the orchestra so that 414–415 is one four-octave D-major climb from the open D to the top
of the E♭ string.

### 16. Animato, the climax (416–437, [M])

Three terraces down, then the hammering: a *ff* tutti descent, the first tune's head over the
tritone pair A♭–D, the descent an octave lower in three octaves, the head again, and then the
whole string body on the G string in unison under the winds' stamps, *fff* from 433. The violin
spends its virtuosic licence on register: the climb from Episode 15 resolves on *G7* at the end
of the fingerboard (the piccolo's sounding pitch) and the first descent is played in octaves
there — the first violins' *G6* on the A string under the piccolo's *G7* on the E♭ — the head an
octave above the first violins, the second descent in octaves an octave lower after the crash,
then the drop of two octaves and a sixth to the open G: three terraces, each an octave apart, the
upper two in octaves as the orchestra doubles them. The top of the A and E♭ strings delivers less
mechanical energy than the middle of the instrument, and that is not the point: the listener's
wonder at the towering height more than makes up for it, and a decent violin in decent hands
makes those notes ring like fire — the novice ear is left with nothing to imagine as to a full
sound. From 426 the figure is a single line on the G string — the
violin's most visceral sound at *fff* — with the flute and piccolo's stamp as a quarter on beat 2
above it, thickening at [M]. The energy of the stringendo's last run goes into one line.

### 17. Dawn (438–454, *Tempo 1°*)

Three events, each alone: the horns' E♭ *fp*, the oboe's cock-crow, the strings' dying tremolo
on C♯°7 under timpani strokes, *f* to *p* to a fermata *très long, toujours en dim.* Each is
lifted at pitch as the one line. The horn note on the D string, the crow entirely on the E♭
string, and the tremolo as the chord's defining interval — the cellos' C♯ against the violas' G,
the *diabolus* that opened the piece — dissolving over ten bars and the long hold. The retuned
string that poisoned midnight is still on the instrument at dawn; every E♮ in the daylight is a
stopped note. The devils do not exit; they evaporate.

### 18. *Déclamé* and coda (455–477, [N])

Death's fiddle speaks its last lines, *f*, over a soft horn chord, then falls to a measured
tremolo on D–E♭ — the summons's two notes — fading to *pp*, while under it the first tune's head
flickers upward three times through violas, second violins and first violins, the basses pluck
the head one last time at the bottom, and the winds place two soft quarter-note chords, D major
then G minor. The declamation is verbatim with the horns' bed as a second voice on the open
strings, held through the solo's breaths as the horns hold. The tremolo is played to 469.1 and
then given up for the three echoes, rising, in one line — the one deliberate departure from the
solo's text. The reason is the listener across the street: the performer hears Saint-Saëns's
whole texture, but an unfamiliar ear catching one violin line should be left with the
orchestra's mysterious last figures, not with a prolonged version of the summons it has already
heard. Then the basses' pizzicato an octave up, and the two final chords plucked — the D-major
and G-minor chords that closed the summons at bars 32–33, quarters with rests exactly as the
winds have them. The piece ends on the plucked open G as it began on the plucked open D.

## 4. Performance notes

**Tuning.** G–D–A–E♭ throughout; everything is written at sounding pitch. Open-E♭-string notes
carry the open-string circle. Every E♮ is a stopped note.

**Pizzicato.** A *pizz.* on a single note or chord applies to that note only; the next note is
bowed and carries no *arco*. A plucked *passage* is marked *pizz. sempre* and runs to an explicit
*arco*. `+` is left-hand pizzicato; small noteheads with `+` are optional left-hand clicks of an
open string under a bowed note (*pizz. m.g. ad lib.*, marked once at bar 34) — the orchestra's
bass G or D, costing nothing. The plucked chords on the beat with the bow back an eighth later
(85–88, 118–128, 312–319) are the Sarasate surprise; practise the retake.

**The three summits.** 172–173.1 (*D♯7* to *E7*), 415 (*F♯7*) and 416–418 (*G7* down to *C7*,
in octaves) are stopped notes at the end of the E♭ string, at *f* and *ff*. They are meant to be
heard as height — make them ring, do not apologise for them. If a single note will
not speak, the fallback is the artificial harmonic in first position (stop a fourth below, touch
a fourth above; the open E♭ touched at *A♭5* gives *E♭7*); if the 416–418 octaves will not, the
fallback is the upper voice alone. No ottava brackets anywhere in the score: the performer sees
the shift.

**Octaves in this tuning.** On the A–E♭ pair an octave is a six-semitone 1–4 stretch and exists
only for the lower note on the A string; everything else lies on D–A or G–D. The octaves at
416–418 and 421–423 are the A–E♭ kind, *ff* quarters. The span shrinks as the hand climbs — about
40 mm at *C6/C7*, under 30 mm at *G6/G7* — so the difficulty at the top is intonation on two
strings at the fingerboard's end, not reach.

**Bowings.** The source's slurs are orchestral bowings and may be re-bowed. The virtuoso bowings
that cost no licence: ricochet or saltato on the whirl (253–264), spiccato on the canon (245–252),
flying staccato on the fugato's counter-lines (145–165), sautillé on the final run (433–437).

**Tempo.** One tempo, the source's, with the Trame's *poco stringendo* at 409 and *Tempo 1°* at
438; the fermata at 454 is *très long*.

Note that the the old version of the score source code was used inadvertently for this project, however, the latest version as of 2026-10 only contains minor and internal changes:
1. The 2020 "v.2" archive is a re-engraving of the parts — not an editorial revision of the music. Across all 35 voice files there are zero changes to pitches, rhythms, bar counts, articulations, dynamics, slurs, tempo/expression text, rehearsal-mark positions, or the scordatura. Nothing in it would alter the arrangement.
2. What actually changed:
  1. LilyPond 2.16 → 2.18 syntax.
  2. -| → -! everywhere. This looked like marcato→staccatissimo but is not: it's LilyPond's own 2.17.11 rename of the staccatissimo shorthand (the | form collided with bar checks). The `convert-ly` upgrade that `build dump` applies already yields -!, and the episodes engrave it as -!. Semantically identical.
  3. Part-extraction cosmetics.
  4. Harp: `\repeat` percent → `\repeat` unfold; its own `\tempo/\time/\key` and `\marks` added because the score wrapper no longer feeds the harp staff from the Trame; everything after m 237 is commented out as "Le reste tacet", so the new harp files are no longer 477 bars.
  5. Clarinets 1–2, mm 205–236: The "A défaut de Harpe" cue (clarinets covering the harp arpeggios when no harp is present) is retagged so it also appears in the score layer via `\partcombineApart`.
  6. `Score.ly`/`Global.ily`: layout, paper spacing, engraver placement, circled measure numbers, "v.2" in the copyright line; the French-language score variant (`00-ScoreFrançais.ly`) was dropped.

The music is public domain; the Le Concert engraving code is CC BY-NC-SA 3.0, and lifted bars were
spliced from it, so the arrangement file is a derivative of that engraving — fine for
non-commercial use with attribution. A clean re-entry would be unencumbered.

The dump's clarinets, horns and trumpets are at *written* pitch; slice them with `--transpose`
(`cl-bb`, `horn-g` to 402, `horn-eb` from 403, `horn-d`, `tp-d`). Every episode file is one
`\absolute` block at sounding pitch, the dump's own convention, so a lift is a paste with no
octave shift.

One script drives the build and the reference tooling:

```
./build                              # score + these notes (PDFs)
./build score                        # output/danse-macabre-solo.pdf only
./build notes                        # output/"Program notes.pdf" only (pandoc + xelatex)
./build slice Cl1 117 136 --transpose cl-bb    # a bar range of one orchestral voice
./build verify                       # every dump is exactly 477 bars of 3/4
./build dump                         # regenerate source/dump from source/lilypond (once, after download)
```

A clean compile reports zero `barNumberCheck` warnings: each episode file checks its first bar and
the next episode's first bar, so the 477-bar form is enforced at every boundary. Do not pass
`-dbackend=null` (it crashes during layout on at least one install, independent of this project).
